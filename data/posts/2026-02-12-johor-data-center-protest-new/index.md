On February 7, 2026, something unprecedented happened in Malaysia. Over 50 residents gathered outside a data center construction site in Gelang Patah, Johor, demanding answers about dust pollution, air quality, and water security. This wasn't a protest led by environmental activists or tech policy experts—it was everyday people whose lives were being directly impacted by the infrastructure powering our digital world.

This was [Malaysia's first AI data center protest](https://www.lowyat.net/2026/381039/malaysia-witnesses-first-ai-data-centre-protest-in-johor/), and it's a wake-up call we all need to hear.

## The Hidden Costs of AI

Every time you use ChatGPT, generate an image with DALL-E, or stream a video, there's physical infrastructure somewhere consuming massive amounts of resources. Data centers—the backbone of our digital lives—are not abstract clouds. They're concrete buildings filled with servers that require enormous amounts of electricity and water to keep running.

And as AI services explode in popularity, so does the demand for these facilities.

## What Happened in Johor?

The protest centered on a hyperscale data center being built by China's Zdata Technologies in Gelang Patah, less than a kilometer from residential homes. Residents reported:

- **Dust pollution** so severe they couldn't dry laundry outside
- **Air quality** readings showing dangerous levels on their home air purifiers
- **Constant construction noise** disrupting daily life
- **Concerns about water security** as the facility would consume massive amounts of water daily

**The water problem is massive.** Data centers require enormous cooling systems to prevent servers from overheating. In Johor, Tier 1 and 2 data centers can consume up to **50 million liters of water per day**—enough to fill 20 Olympic-sized swimming pools. This is why Johor [halted approvals for new Tier 1 and 2 data centers](https://www.lowyat.net/2025/374704/johor-data-centre-approvals/) in 2025, though the Zdata project received approval before this ban.

**Why Can't They Just Use Seawater?**

You might wonder: "Why not use seawater? We're near the ocean!" Unfortunately, it's not that simple. Data centers require **potable (drinkable) water** or highly treated water for their cooling systems. Here's why:

- **Corrosion and Mineral Buildup**: Seawater contains salt and minerals that corrode pipes, heat exchangers, and cooling equipment. This leads to expensive maintenance, equipment failure, and reduced efficiency.
- **Biological Growth**: Seawater contains microorganisms that can form biofilms inside cooling systems, clogging pipes and reducing heat transfer efficiency.
- **Desalination Costs**: While you can desalinate seawater, the process is energy-intensive and expensive—adding to both operational costs and environmental impact.
- **System Design**: Most cooling systems are designed for freshwater. Retrofitting for seawater requires specialized materials (like titanium) that significantly increase costs.

Some data centers use "gray water" (recycled wastewater) or seawater cooling in specific configurations, but these require substantial additional infrastructure. The default remains: data centers compete directly with residents for the same freshwater supply.

This is why the Gelang Patah residents' concerns about water security aren't just about quantity—they're about **competing for a finite resource** that communities need for drinking, cooking, and daily life.

## Singapore's Approach: Regulations Matter

Singapore, just across the causeway from Johor, also hosts numerous data centers. But there's a key difference: **strict regulations.**

The Infocomm Media Development Authority (IMDA) has established comprehensive frameworks including:

- [**Green Data Centre Standard**](https://www.imda.gov.sg/regulations-and-licensing-listing/ict-standards-and-quality-of-service/it-standards-and-frameworks/green-data-centre-standard): Guidelines for energy efficiency, cooling optimization, and environmental sustainability
- [**Advisory Guidelines for Cloud Services and Data Centres**](https://www.imda.gov.sg/resources/press-releases-factsheets-and-speeches/press-releases/2025/advisory-guidelines-of-cloud-services-and-data-centres): Requirements for security, resilience, and operational standards

These regulations make Singapore's data center industry more sustainable—but also more expensive to operate. This regulatory difference is precisely what makes Malaysia, particularly Johor, so attractive to data center developers.

### Why Build Data Centers in Malaysia and Singapore?

From a technical standpoint, the Singapore-Malaysia region is a strategic hub for data center development. If you've ever wondered why tech giants are racing to build here, the answer lies in fundamental principles that software engineers, DevOps engineers, solutions architects, cloud architects, and site reliability engineers (SREs) deal with daily.

**Latency Matters—A Lot**

When you're deploying applications or architecting cloud solutions, one of the first rules is: **choose a data center as close to your users as possible.** Every millisecond counts.

Here's why:
- **User Experience**: A 100ms delay can reduce conversion rates by 7%. For e-commerce or financial services, that's millions in lost revenue.
- **Real-time Applications**: Video calls, gaming, and trading platforms require ultra-low latency. The further your data travels, the more lag users experience.
- **API Response Times**: Modern applications make dozens of API calls per page load. If your backend is in the US but your users are in Southeast Asia, each call adds 200-300ms of latency.

Southeast Asia has over 700 million people and is one of the world's fastest-growing digital markets. To serve this region effectively, you need data centers **in** the region.

**Data Sovereignty and Compliance**

Solutions architects and cloud architects also consider **data residency requirements**:
- Many countries require certain data (especially personal data, financial records, or health information) to be stored within national borders
- Singapore is a trusted jurisdiction with strong data protection laws
- Malaysia offers proximity while being a separate legal jurisdiction, providing redundancy options

**The Technical Sweet Spot**

For roles like DevOps engineers and SREs managing multi-region deployments, the Singapore-Malaysia corridor offers:
- **Geographic redundancy**: Place primary infrastructure in Singapore, disaster recovery in Johor—close enough for low latency, far enough to avoid single points of failure
- **Network connectivity**: Singapore is one of the world's most connected cities, with extensive submarine cable networks
- **Availability zones**: Multiple data centers in close proximity enable high-availability architectures

### Malaysia's Strategic Position

This technical reality, combined with regulatory differences, makes Malaysia—particularly Johor—an attractive alternative. Companies get:
- Proximity to Singapore's highly connected digital infrastructure (crucial for low-latency requirements)
- Lower operational costs
- Fewer regulatory hurdles
- Access to the same regional market
- Geographic redundancy for disaster recovery strategies

By mid-2025, Johor had approved **RM164.45 billion** (S$53.2 billion) in data center investments, attracting tech giants like Amazon, Oracle, ByteDance, and Alibaba. The state became [Southeast Asia's fastest-growing data center hub](https://www.businesstimes.com.sg/international/asean/malaysia-draws-first-data-centre-protest-over-pollution-water).

But this growth came at a cost that residents are now refusing to pay silently.

## A Global Movement

The Gelang Patah protest isn't an isolated incident. Around the world, communities are pushing back against data center developments:

### The Monterey Park Success Story

In California, just **five residents** in Monterey Park successfully stopped a massive data center project in just six weeks. Their strategy? They organized quickly, educated themselves about the project's impacts, and mobilized their community. [The Guardian covered their victory](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/feb/07/california-monterey-park-stop-datacenter-construction), calling it a David-versus-Goliath story that shows what's possible when communities act.

### The Numbers Are Staggering

According to [Truthout's reporting](https://truthout.org/articles/as-towns-and-cities-fight-off-data-centers-calls-grow-for-a-national-moratorium/), between March and June 2025, communities worldwide cancelled or delayed **$98 billion worth of data center projects**. Two-thirds of targeted projects were halted or significantly modified.

These protests are happening because:
- **Water scarcity** is becoming critical in many regions
- **Energy consumption** is straining local grids
- **Environmental impact** is undeniable
- **Community consultation** is often minimal or absent

And crucially, these protests are being led by ordinary residents—not tech experts or environmental activists. People are learning how the technology that shapes their lives actually works, and they're demanding accountability.

## Why This Matters for Everyone

The Johor protest represents something bigger than one data center in one Malaysian state. It's about:

1. **Tech Literacy as Power**: When people understand how technology works—and what it costs—they can make informed decisions and hold companies accountable.

2. **Environmental Justice**: The benefits of AI and cloud computing are distributed globally, but the environmental costs are concentrated in specific communities. That's not equitable.

3. **Democratic Participation**: Technology policy shouldn't be decided only by tech companies and governments. Communities have a right to participate in decisions that affect their lives.

4. **Sustainable Development**: We can build the digital infrastructure we need without destroying the environment or displacing communities. But only if we demand it.
