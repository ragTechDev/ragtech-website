# Johor Data Center Protest - Long-Form Teleprompter Script

**Host:** Natasha

Hey everyone, I'm Natasha, co-host of ragTech—a tech podcast and initiative started by real people in tech about real life in tech. We're here to make technology accessible and understandable for everyone, not just engineers and developers.

Today, I want to talk about something that happened last week that I think is incredibly important. On February 7th, 2026, Malaysia witnessed its first-ever data center protest. Over 50 residents gathered outside a construction site in Gelang Patah, Johor, demanding answers about dust pollution, air quality, and water security.

Now, you might be thinking, "Why should I care about a protest in Malaysia?" But here's the thing—this story is about so much more than one data center in one country. It's about the hidden costs of the technology we use every day, about environmental justice, and about how ordinary people are learning to hold tech companies accountable.

So let's dive in.

First, let me set the scene. Gelang Patah is a district in Johor, the southernmost state of Malaysia, right across the causeway from Singapore. It's a rapidly developing area that's become a hotspot for data center construction.

The protest was directed at a hyperscale data center being built by Zdata Technologies, a Chinese company. And these weren't professional activists or environmental groups leading the charge—these were everyday residents whose lives were being directly impacted.

What were their concerns?

Dust pollution. Residents reported that construction dust was so severe they couldn't dry their laundry outside. Imagine not being able to use your own balcony because everything gets covered in a layer of dust within hours.

Air quality. People's home air purifiers were constantly showing dangerous levels. We're talking about the air inside their homes becoming unhealthy because of what's happening outside.

Noise. Constant construction noise disrupting daily life, making it hard to sleep, hard to work from home, hard to just exist peacefully in your own space.

But the biggest concern? Water.

Here's something most people don't realize about data centers: they consume absolutely massive amounts of water. We're not talking about a little bit—we're talking about industrial-scale water consumption.

In Johor, Tier 1 and 2 data centers can consume up to 50 million liters of water per day. Let me put that in perspective: that's enough to fill 20 Olympic-sized swimming pools. Every. Single. Day.

Why do they need so much water? Cooling. Data centers are essentially giant buildings filled with thousands of servers running 24/7. All those servers generate enormous amounts of heat. If you don't cool them properly, they overheat and fail. So data centers use massive cooling systems, and many of those systems rely on water.

Now, you might be thinking, "Okay, but Johor is near the ocean. Why not just use seawater?"

Great question. And this is where it gets technical. Data centers need potable water—drinkable water—or at least highly treated water. Here's why:

Corrosion. Seawater contains salt and minerals that corrode pipes, heat exchangers, and cooling equipment. This leads to expensive maintenance, equipment failure, and reduced efficiency.

Biological growth. Seawater contains microorganisms that form biofilms inside cooling systems, clogging pipes and reducing heat transfer efficiency.

Desalination costs. Yes, you can desalinate seawater, but the process is energy-intensive and expensive. It adds to both operational costs and environmental impact.

System design. Most cooling systems are designed for freshwater. Retrofitting for seawater requires specialized materials like titanium that significantly increase costs.

Some data centers do use gray water—recycled wastewater—or seawater cooling in specific configurations, but these require substantial additional infrastructure. The default remains: data centers compete directly with residents for the same freshwater supply.

And this is why the Gelang Patah residents' concerns about water security aren't just about quantity—they're about competing for a finite resource that communities need for drinking, cooking, and daily life.

In fact, the water situation got so serious that in November 2025, Johor halted approvals for new Tier 1 and 2 data centers specifically because of water consumption concerns. The Zdata project got approved before this ban, but the ban itself shows that even the government recognized this was becoming unsustainable.

So why are all these data centers being built in Malaysia, and specifically in Johor? To understand this, we need to talk about some technical concepts that software engineers, DevOps engineers, solutions architects, and cloud architects deal with every day.

Latency.

When you're deploying applications or architecting cloud solutions, one of the first rules is: choose a data center as close to your users as possible. Every millisecond counts.

A 100-millisecond delay can reduce conversion rates by 7%. For e-commerce or financial services, that's millions in lost revenue. Real-time applications like video calls, gaming, and trading platforms require ultra-low latency. The further your data travels, the more lag users experience.

Southeast Asia has over 700 million people and is one of the world's fastest-growing digital markets. To serve this region effectively, you need data centers in the region.

Data sovereignty.

Many countries require certain data—especially personal data, financial records, or health information—to be stored within national borders. Singapore is a trusted jurisdiction with strong data protection laws. Malaysia offers proximity while being a separate legal jurisdiction, providing redundancy options.

Geographic redundancy.

For DevOps engineers and site reliability engineers managing multi-region deployments, the Singapore-Malaysia corridor offers the perfect setup. You can place primary infrastructure in Singapore and disaster recovery in Johor—close enough for low latency, far enough to avoid single points of failure.

The regulatory difference.

Now here's the key part: Singapore has strict regulations for data centers. The Infocomm Media Development Authority has established comprehensive frameworks including the Green Data Centre Standard and Advisory Guidelines for Cloud Services and Data Centres.

These regulations make Singapore's data center industry more sustainable—but also more expensive to operate.

Malaysia, particularly Johor, offers the same geographic advantages—proximity to Singapore, access to submarine cable networks, low latency to the region—but with fewer regulatory hurdles and lower operational costs.

By mid-2025, Johor had approved RM164.45 billion—that's about 53 billion Singapore dollars—in data center investments. Tech giants like Amazon, Oracle, ByteDance, and Alibaba are all building there. Johor became Southeast Asia's fastest-growing data center hub.

But this growth came at a cost that residents are now refusing to pay silently.

Here's what makes the Johor protest even more significant: it's not an isolated incident. Around the world, communities are pushing back against data center developments.

Let me tell you about Monterey Park, California. Just five residents—five people—successfully stopped a massive data center project in just six weeks. How? They organized quickly, educated themselves about the project's impacts, and mobilized their community. The Guardian called it a David-versus-Goliath story that shows what's possible when communities act.

And the numbers are staggering. Between March and June 2025, communities worldwide cancelled or delayed 98 billion dollars worth of data center projects. Two-thirds of targeted projects were halted or significantly modified.

Why is this happening?

Water scarcity is becoming critical in many regions. Energy consumption is straining local grids. Environmental impact is undeniable. And crucially, community consultation is often minimal or absent.

What's particularly interesting is who's leading these protests. These aren't tech experts or professional environmental activists. These are ordinary residents—people who are learning how the technology that shapes their lives actually works, and they're demanding accountability.

This is tech literacy in action. When people understand how data centers work, why they consume so much water and energy, what the alternatives are, and how regulations can make a difference—they're empowered to ask the right questions and demand better practices.

So why should you care about this? Why does a protest in Johor matter to someone watching this in New York or London or Tokyo?

Because this is about more than data centers. It's about how we build the digital infrastructure that powers our lives.

Environmental justice. The benefits of AI and cloud computing are distributed globally. We all get to use ChatGPT, stream Netflix, store files in the cloud. But the environmental costs—the water consumption, the energy use, the pollution—are concentrated in specific communities. That's not equitable.

Democratic participation. Technology policy shouldn't be decided only by tech companies and governments. Communities have a right to participate in decisions that affect their lives. When a data center is built in your neighborhood, you should have a say in whether and how it's built.

Sustainable development. We can build the digital infrastructure we need without destroying the environment or displacing communities. But only if we demand it. There are sustainable cooling technologies that reduce water consumption by 20%. There are renewable energy commitments. There are transparent environmental impact assessments. These aren't impossible standards—they're industry best practices that some companies are already implementing.

Tech literacy as power. This is the core of what we do at ragTech. We believe that technology should be accessible and understandable for everyone. When you understand how the technology shaping your world actually works, you can hold companies accountable. You can participate in policy discussions. You can make informed decisions.

The Johor protest shows what happens when people gain that understanding. They didn't need to be engineers or developers to recognize that something was wrong. They educated themselves, organized, and demanded answers.

So what's the path forward?

The question isn't whether we should have data centers—it's how we build them responsibly, who bears the costs, and who gets to decide.

For residents affected by data center development: Document everything. Air quality readings, water pressure changes, construction impacts. Organize with your neighbors. Demand transparency from officials and companies. Learn from other communities like Monterey Park that have successfully pushed back.

For tech professionals: Ask questions about the infrastructure powering your applications. Advocate for responsible practices within your organization. Support community-led efforts to ensure sustainable development. Share your knowledge to help non-technical people understand what's at stake.
