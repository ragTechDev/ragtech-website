# The Dilemma
- Caught in between the need to champion women empowerment by using and leveraging AI, and needing to champion the ethics/responsibility of it given AI's impact
- In the United States, surveys indicate that 50% of men use popular AI tools compared to just 37% of women. This gap persists even within the same occupations, with women 16 percentage points less likely to incorporate AI into job tasks. The disparity reflects heightened awareness of risks. Women cite privacy concerns as a primary deterrent, showing greater wariness about data misuse in AI interactions. They prioritize issues like AI hallucinations, inherent biases, and potential job displacement more than men. Studies reveal AI chatbots recommend lower salaries for women than for men with identical profiles, perpetuating wage gaps. (https://www.artiba.org/blog/how-women-ai-leaders-turn-risk-awareness-into-technical-excellence)

# Intentional AI
- For me, my balance is using AI intentionally. I use it to help me with my work, but I also make sure to use it responsibly and ethically. Every single time I use it, I'm not using it mindlessly, but weighing the scale of impact of my work with it versus the known costs of it. any unnecessary use, i do not use it. where I do use it, i use it optimally
- I have never used image or video generation. even as someone who posts visual content every single day (social media, tech podcast). i innovate ways to generate visual graphics, like using SVG or HTML code, way more lightweight. I use AI to clean up content, like improving scripts, repurpose long form content into short form. but i still film my own videos, draw our own graphics where we need to. see https://futurenet.ragtechdev.com/, our initiative for children's digital space - all the doodle-like illustrations are code generated (SVG based).
- everything i do, i try to do it in code. i write my posts in code, my workflows and plans are contained in code repositories. so i never have to face an issue of spending more ai compute to translate word docs into something else, pdfs into smth else. and context is never wasted, they're all conntained in a code base. this is what i call business as code. 
- i have not needed to use agents either because of this workflow. i can code up specific automatable workflows in my codebase, like email automation, content generation, etc. i even have a content repurposing tool that converts long form content into short form content. never needed to use agents, because i prefer to define the rules through code so i never lose control or face the problem of agents interpreting my message wrongly, like when openclaw deleted messages in meta's head of ai safety's email inbox (insert source article).
- i make sure i never generate the same thing twice. engineering mindset, everything is a system, templatized. if i know i have to keep generating the same thing, i use an ai instead to code a system for it. for example, our blogposts being sent out and reformatted as newsletter on our tech podcast. i could use ai to turn my blogpost into email format every single time. but instead i wrote a codified script with ai to turn it into email format once, and then i can reuse it for every single blogpost.
- i also make sure i never use ai to generate content that is not mine. i use it to help me with my work, but i also make sure to use it responsibly and ethically. the line i draw is this: if it's my work, i can use ai to help me with it. if it's not my work, i cannot use ai to generate it. by avoiding generating images and videos, i've already evaded a huge bulk of this ethical dilemma, noting that image and video generation takes from the work of artists without royalty. even if i had to use it, the most i would use it for it to generate a draft for inspiration, and then use ai to teach my how i can recreate it for myself by learning skills. 
- when it comes to writing, i write all the points in a single train of thought. all thoughts are mine. and i do research on my own too, still using traditional search engine searches to read through articles myself and verify information based on my own judgment. i only use ai to clean up language and sharpen prose, and i insert articles and sources for it to refer to. in that way, i maintain complete ownership of my work while still leveraging ai's strengths
- code is probably the least unethical, because it is trained with open source code - which by the coder's intention, they already meant for it to be used by public. hence i default most things i do to its code native. and code is explicit - there isn't room to play around with word choices or diction to express self. it is systematic, which i think is where ai thrives. i use plantuml or mermaid code to generate planning diagrams, html for reports - slideshows, and everything i do has a website. for example when i send a pitch deck to someone, it's usually a website instead of a pdf pitch deck. i save on data transfer costs in the data center, and the need to use ai to generate the full deck.
- and lastly, i use it to its fullest strengths, and educate myself and others about its pitfalls. if ight for regulation around it. because in the ideal world, i want to be able to use ai without guilt, without negative impact. i want ai to be a tool to empower women, without it being something that takes away from others.
- this is where i see how women can come in. we are naturally community centered (cite research if possible), and when we do use a tool, we find ways to life our community with us. we need more women rising up to being in the diffult space of wrangling with the ethical dilemmas of usage while maximizing our work.

# Women Form Communities on Ethical AI; Women are Whistleblowers
- Feifei Li: "I believe in human-centered AI to benefit people in positive and benevolent ways. It is deeply against my principles to work on any project that I believe weaponize AI." AI4ALL, a nonprofit educating diverse AI leaders. The mentorship embedded in AI4ALL extends her impact beyond individual research into systemic change. (https://www.artiba.org/blog/how-women-ai-leaders-turn-risk-awareness-into-technical-excellence)
- Joy Buolamwini founded the Algorithmic Justice League to challenge bias in decision-making software. Inspired by MIT's Kismet robot at age nine, she taught herself programming while competing as an athlete. After graduating from Georgia Tech and working as a Fulbright fellow in Zambia, her 2017 Gender Shades project at MIT revealed intersectional biases in facial recognition systems. (https://www.artiba.org/blog/how-women-ai-leaders-turn-risk-awareness-into-technical-excellence)
- Timnit Gebru, was co-lead of the Ethical AI research team before she was fired for her paper on stochastic parrots, co-founder of Black in AI, an advocacy group that has pushed for more Black roles in AI development and research (https://www.dair-institute.org/team/timnit-gebru/) 
- Frances Haugen, advocate for accountability & transparency in social media, In 2019, she was recruited to Facebook to be the lead Product Manager on the Civic Misinformation team, which dealt with issues related to democracy and misinformation, and later also worked on counter-espionage. During her time at Facebook, Frances became increasingly alarmed by the choices the company makes prioritizing their own profits over public safety and putting people's lives at risk. As a last resort and at great personal risk, Frances made the courageous decision to blow the whistle on Facebook. The initial reporting was done by the Wall Street Journal in what became known as “The Facebook Files”. (https://www.franceshaugen.com/)
- Kate Crawford is one of the foremost global voices on AI governance and ethics. A Senior Principal Researcher at Microsoft Research and co-founder of the AI Now Institute at NYU, her work explores the social, political, and environmental consequences of artificial intelligence. Her award-winning book, Atlas of AI (2021) revealed the hidden costs of AI systems, from labor exploitation to climate impact. Crawford stands out among women in AI governance for her insistence on transparency and accountability in how technology shapes society. (https://businesswomen.com/blog/women-in-ai-you-should-know/)

# Women are building apps with ai for societal problems
- Rana el Kaliouby co-founded Affectiva to develop emotion AI for mental health tools, demonstrating how AI can enhance wellbeing with clear ethical guidelines about consent and privacy. Mentorship from leaders like Rus and el Kaliouby operates at multiple levels: technical machine learning skills, critical thinking about appropriate applications, communication abilities, ethical evaluation frameworks, and resilience for navigating male-dominated fields. (https://www.artiba.org/blog/how-women-ai-leaders-turn-risk-awareness-into-technical-excellence)
- Daniela Rus leads MIT's Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, advancing soft robotics for disaster response applications. Her leadership creates environments where emerging researchers learn technical skills and ethical frameworks simultaneously. This integration matters because ethical considerations can't be bolted onto AI after development. They need to inform initial design decisions. (https://www.artiba.org/blog/how-women-ai-leaders-turn-risk-awareness-into-technical-excellence)

As one of the most visionary AI entrepreneurs, Koller proves how women in AI are driving innovation that solves real-world problems and improves people’s lives.
