Some of you may remember our previous episodes, when we couldn't stop talking about how amazing AI was for productivity.

Well... things got complicated since then.

In our latest episode of ragTech, the three of us—Natasha (software engineer), Saloni (software developer), and Victoria (solutions engineer)—sat down to talk about something we didn't expect to be discussing: *burnout from using AI*.

The irony isn't lost on us. We've spent months advocating for AI tools, showing people how they can boost productivity and streamline workflows. But somewhere along the way, each of us independently started pulling back. We only realized we were all doing it when we finally talked about it together.

# The Honeymoon Phase is Over
Let's rewind a bit. When we first started using AI tools, they felt like magic. Victoria was cranking out technical blog posts faster than ever (she's been blogging about tech on lo-victoria.com since 2020 by the way!). Saloni was building features at lightning speed. The productivity gains were real, measurable, and honestly, pretty intoxicating.

But we found out the hard way that *there's a hidden cost to all that efficiency*.

# Victoria's Writing Crisis
Victoria used to write weekly blog posts—every single week, for years. She had 7-8 drafts in her backlog, each one carefully crafted from real experiences and insights. Writing was her way of processing what she learned, and she could remember every article she'd ever written.

> I can remember what I write [wrote] even years ago. When my readers refer to an article, I immediately know that's the one I wrote in 2022. I remember every word I wrote, and I remember how I struggled to think about how to get it out of my brain. - *Victoria, on how she remembers her writing pre-AI*

"I can remember what I write [wrote] even years ago," she said. "When my readers refer to an article, I immediately know that's the one I wrote in 2022. I remember every word I wrote, and I remember how I struggled to think about how to get it out of my brain."

Then AI came along. Suddenly, she could pump out articles faster. But the pressure to keep up with everyone else who was also using AI became overwhelming. She started writing about topics she wasn't even passionate about, letting AI do the research and heavy lifting.

And unfortunately she realized... *She couldn't remember what she'd written.* The struggle—the part that made writing meaningful—was gone. The "you" in the writing had disappeared.

"There's no you in it, you know," Natasha pointed out.

Now Victoria's on what she calls an "AI detox." She's writing everything from scratch again. It takes longer—sometimes one or two days instead of four hours—but she's rediscovering why she loved writing in the first place.

# Saloni's Coding Conundrum
Saloni's story is not necessarily about writing, but on coding. As someone who's run workshops on using AI for software engineering, she was all-in on the technology. AI is REALLY good at writing code.

But all of a sudden, Saloni realized... "I lost my passion for coding," she admitted. "I'm not coding anymore. The AI does the coding for me."

Sure, she's become more of a solutions architect—breaking down tasks, orchestrating different AI tools, reviewing code. But the joy? The creative puzzle-solving that makes coding fun? That's gone.

"There's no joy in reviewing," she said. "There's joy in writing code. I want to write 'public static void main.' I don't know when I last wrote it."

# The Creative Process Matters
Here's what we're all realizing: *coding and writing aren't just about the end result*. They're creative processes. The struggle, the thinking, the problem-solving—that's not just busywork. That's where the learning happens. That's where the satisfaction comes from.

When you outsource that struggle to AI, you might get faster results, but you lose something essential. You lose the retention. You lose the pride. You lose the "you" in your work.

# Finding the Balance
So are we saying AI is bad? No. That would be just as extreme as our initial "AI is amazing for everything" phase.

What we're learning is that *AI works best for things we genuinely don't want to do*. The truly mundane tasks. The boilerplate. The stuff that doesn't require our unique perspective or creativity.

But when you start using AI for things you used to love, that's when you need to pause and ask yourself: *Am I using this tool, or is it using me?*

# The Side Hustle Sweet Spot
Interestingly, we all agreed that AI has been a game-changer for side projects. Those ideas you never had time to build? Now you can actually bring them to life. AI can help you move from concept to reality faster than ever before.

The difference? These are projects where the vision matters more than the implementation details. You're not trying to enjoy the coding process—you're trying to see if your idea works.

# Skills Are Eroding (And That's Scary)
When you rely on AI too much, your skills atrophy. Victoria noticed she couldn't write as quickly or as well without AI anymore. Her writing muscles had weakened.

"I realized that my own skills have eroded," she said. "And it's not a good feeling."

This is the part that should worry all of us. If we're not careful, we could end up dependent on tools that might not always be there, or that might change in ways we don't control.

# What We're Doing Differently Now
We're all experimenting with our own versions of balance:

- *Being selective*: Using AI for tasks that are genuinely mundane, not for work we find meaningful

- *Taking breaks*: Victoria's AI detox is helping her rebuild her writing skills

- *Staying hands-on*: Making sure we're still doing enough manual work to maintain our core skills

- *Checking in with ourselves*: Asking regularly whether AI is serving us or whether we're serving it

# The Bigger Picture
As AI tools get better and more integrated into our workflows, these questions are only going to get more important. We're in uncharted territory here, and there's no playbook for getting it right.

But here's what we know: *technology should enhance our humanity, not replace it*. When AI starts making us feel less creative, less skilled, or less connected to our work, something's wrong.

The goal isn't to reject AI entirely, but to use it intentionally, thoughtfully, and in ways that preserve what makes our work meaningful.

# Intentional AI (Psst.. We did a workshop on it!)
![Intentional AI Workshop by Natasha at STTGDC](https://media.licdn.com/dms/image/v2/D5622AQGBjcx0o-x1eQ/feedshare-shrink_2048_1536/B56Zrnk48PLMA0-/0/1764821799652?e=1778112000&v=beta&t=KOb6zT1XQmldu6Vs46D69lMmSiGe_OoI29P1uASzlPY)

That's why Natasha has been doing workshops for companies on what she called *"Intentional AI."* She's been sharing her insights on how to use AI intentionally, thoughtfully, and in ways that preserve what makes our work meaningful.

In her *last workshop at ST Telemedia Global Data Centers*, she handwrote various applications of AI on cue cards that ranged from "Using AI to auto-draft executive summaries" to "Using AI for a team bonding poster," and passed them around the team at STT GDC.

![Participants discussing at the Intentional AI Workshop by Natasha at STTGDC](https://media.licdn.com/dms/image/v2/D5622AQE3czRd00Jw9w/feedshare-shrink_2048_1536/B56Zrnk48RMAAw-/0/1764821799796?e=1778112000&v=beta&t=SZPHG_ppIe2rSNPRMw-Ev2__g6EqFhyH6zjG7K5nRoI)
